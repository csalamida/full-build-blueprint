# Guided interview before the full blueprint

Use for a new full blueprint or a substantially changed process. Read available sources first. This is a question bank, not a script to recite. Skip answered or irrelevant topics, and ask follow-ups that fit the actual business.

## Keep it easy to answer

- Ask one to three questions per turn, on one related topic where possible.
- Explain why an answer matters when the reason is not obvious.
- Give a concrete example. Ask “What tells us the call actually happened?” rather than “What is the completion event?”
- Offer short choices only when the choices are genuinely useful. Preserve the user's free answer.
- Wait for the reply before choosing the next questions. Never treat elapsed time as an answer.
- Do useful source reading and planning while answers are pending, but do not invent the missing business decision.
- Accept “I don't know” and “ask the client”. Label that item and identify what it affects. Do not loop on the same question.
- Do not ask for credentials in chat. Ask who can grant access and how it will be provided securely.
- Keep known facts, suggested choices and unanswered questions separate. Record corrections; do not revert to an earlier assumption later.

For a long interview, keep a concise private note in the user's working area when appropriate: confirmed answers, source references, suggestions, missing answers/owners, affected stages and next questions. Do not save client answers in this public skill repository. Tell the user where a note was saved. Resume from it instead of starting again.

## Round 1 — the business and desired result

Resolve the basic scope before detailed settings:

- What does the business do, and which process are we improving?
- What is difficult today? Which parts already work and should stay?
- What should staff be able to do once the setup is finished?
- Which CRM/tools are already in use? Which are chosen versus merely being considered?
- Who will operate the system, decide the rules and approve the result?

Example opening when sources already identify the tools:

“I can see you use HighLevel and OneDrive and want n8n for the connections. Before I map the stages: which daily job causes the most chasing, and what would a successful setup change for the team?”

## Round 2 — the actual journey

Walk through one real example from start to finish. Keep existing stages where agreed; if no stages exist, suggest a provisional list grounded in the example.

- Where does the first enquiry come from, and who deals with it?
- What happens next, and what must be received or completed first?
- For each stage: who acts, what do they use, and what proves it is finished?
- Which actions should be automatic, which stay manual, and which need both?
- What happens with no reply, rejection, cancellation, reopening or a person asking about more than one thing?

Do not repeat the same generic questions for every stage if the answer is shared. Follow the stage until its entry, actions, finish and next handoff are clear.

## Round 3 — people, records and source material

- Can one person have several businesses, jobs or enquiries? Which information must remain separate?
- What should staff see in each record and daily work list?
- Which forms, contracts, letters, emails and files already exist? Which are final and approved?
- Who owns each file, how is the right version selected, and who is allowed to open it?
- Which records/history need to move from an older system, and what can stay there?
- What original examples can prove each incoming form/email route?

Separate materials already received from items still required. Actual tool capability, account permissions and source-data feasibility are checks for the builder, not questions the client must guess at.

## Round 4 — the small settings

Use only relevant parts of the small-details review. Ask about concrete choices such as:

- Calls: timezone, duration, hours, busy calendar, notice/buffers, who calls whom and cancellation rules.
- Messages: sender, reply owner, wording, appearance, reminder timing/count and stopping rules.
- Signing/payments: approved versions, signers/order, correct invoice, bank-transfer checks and release conditions.
- Files: folder layout, naming, approved customer-facing file and private staff files.
- Staff work: assignment, cover, due dates and who receives problems.

Group shared choices once. For example, approve the sender and appearance for the system, then ask only about messages that need different treatment. Do not introduce SMS, payments, AI features or marketing campaigns solely because they appear in a checklist.

## Round 5 — exceptions and practical limits

Give a short scenario, then ask how the business wants to handle it:

- “The same person asks about two businesses. Should each enquiry move separately?”
- “The enquiry doesn't identify a business clearly. Who should review it?”
- “The agreement is signed but the approved pack is missing. Who prepares it?”
- “A client replies while reminders are running. Which reminders should stop?”
- “A booking changes or staff is away. Who takes over?”

The builder should propose sensible handling for repeated events, failed sends and expired connections, explain the staff-facing result and flag account tests. Do not force clients to design retry algorithms or database rules.

## Round 6 — recap and readiness

Summarise the daily routine and material decisions. Explain any remaining unknowns, their owners and the stages they affect. Distinguish:

- Information needed to finish the plan.
- Access/files/account checks needed before project kickoff.
- Reviews and launch approval that happen after the system is built.

Once the material business choices are clear, draft the full plan and map. If the user wants the document with open items, make a working draft with those items visible. Do not hold the entire plan hostage to a minor unknown, but do not describe it as ready to build while a material question or necessary account check remains unresolved.
